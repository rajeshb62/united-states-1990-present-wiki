Elon Musk (b. 1971)

Elon Reeve Musk is an engineer-entrepreneur active in the United States from the mid-1990s onward. Ashlee Vance’s 2015 biography presents him as a figure who converted early Internet wealth into hardware companies: Space Exploration Technologies (SpaceX), founded in June 2002, and Tesla Motors, which he joined as largest shareholder and chairman in 2004 and later led as CEO. By the early 2010s Vance depicts Musk splitting his week between SpaceX’s Hawthorne factory and Tesla’s Palo Alto offices and Fremont plant, while also serving as chairman of SolarCity.

Origins and arrival in North America

Musk was born on 28 June 1971 in Pretoria, South Africa, to Errol Musk (mechanical and electrical engineer) and Maye Musk (née Haldeman; model and dietician). He had a younger brother, Kimbal, and a sister, Tosca. Vance treats the Pretoria childhood—boarding school, engineering tinkering, and the decision to leave apartheid-era South Africa—as prologue to the American career. Musk arrived in Canada in 1988 via a Canadian citizenship path, worked odd jobs, and enrolled at Queen’s University in Kingston, Ontario, in 1989. In 1992 he transferred to the University of Pennsylvania on scholarship, pursuing an economics degree from the Wharton School and a bachelor’s degree in physics.

After Penn he briefly entered a Stanford materials-science and physics graduate track, then left after two days in 1995 for the early Web. That summer he and Kimbal drove across the United States and settled on a Silicon Valley software idea that became Zip2.

Zip2 (1995–1999)

In 1995 the brothers formed Global Link Information Network in Palo Alto; the firm was soon renamed Zip2. The product began as a searchable online directory and city-guide software for businesses, later sold as publishing software to newspapers under a “We Power the Press” strategy. Venture capital (notably Mohr Davidow) arrived in early 1996; Musk lost day-to-day CEO control as professional managers took over. A proposed merger with CitySearch collapsed in May 1998. In February 1999 Compaq Computer bought Zip2 for 307millionincash.Muskreceivedabout22 million; Kimbal about $15 million. Vance stresses that Musk thereafter tried to keep control of companies he built.

X.com, PayPal, and the exit (1999–2002)

Before the Zip2 sale closed, Musk began planning an online bank. In March 1999 he incorporated X.com in Palo Alto and put most of his Zip2 proceeds into it, leaving roughly 4millionforpersonaluseaftertaxes.X.comlaunchedperson−to−personpaymentsandcompetedwithConfinity’sPayPalproduct.InMarch2000thefirmsmergedundertheX.comname;shortlyafterwardthecombinedcompanyraised100 million. Board and brand fights followed. While Musk was on a honeymoon flight after marrying Justine Wilson in January 2000, executives delivered letters of no confidence; he was removed as CEO. In June 2001 X.com was rebranded PayPal. Musk remained the largest shareholder. In July 2002 eBay acquired PayPal for $1.5 billion. The alumni network around that exit became known as the “PayPal Mafia.”

Space and Los Angeles (2001–2002)

Turning thirty in June 2001, Musk moved from Silicon Valley toward Los Angeles and space. He joined the Mars Society’s board, donated to its projects, and explored a publicity stunt involving mice and Mars before abandoning that plan. In April 2002, with PayPal’s public-market and acquisition trajectory enlarging his fortune, he committed to a commercial rocket company. Space Exploration Technologies was founded in June 2002 in an El Segundo warehouse. Vance reports that Musk put on the order of $100 million into SpaceX over the following years.

Tesla and the dual industrial bet (2004–2008)

Martin Eberhard and Marc Tarpenning incorporated Tesla Motors on 1 July 2003. In 2004 Musk invested $6.5 million, becoming largest shareholder and chairman; J. B. Straubel joined as an early engineer that May. Tesla pursued an all-electric sports car (the Roadster) using a Lotus-based chassis and lithium-ion battery packs. Production delays, cost overruns, and board conflict led to Eberhard’s demotion in August 2007 and departure by December; interim CEO Michael Marks and then Ze’ev Drori followed. Musk took over as Tesla CEO in October 2008.

The same season SpaceX’s Falcon 1 failed on its third Kwajalein attempt (2 August 2008) after earlier failures in March 2006 and March 2007. On 28 September 2008 the fourth Falcon 1 reached orbit—the first privately built machine to do so, in Vance’s account. Tesla and SpaceX both faced cash crises as the financial markets froze. Musk sold assets, took loans, and nearly exhausted his liquid wealth. Tesla’s rescue financing closed on Christmas Eve 2008; on 23 December 2008 NASA awarded SpaceX a $1.6 billion contract for twelve International Space Station resupply flights. Personally, Musk filed for divorce from Justine in June 2008 and later married and divorced actress Talulah Riley (Vance’s photographic apparatus notes the cycle of remarriage and divorce).

Liftoff years (2009–2015)

After 2008, SpaceX developed Falcon 9 and the Dragon capsule from a larger Hawthorne headquarters. Falcon 9 first flew successfully in June 2010; in December 2010 Falcon 9 lofted Dragon and recovered it after ocean landing. In May 2012 Dragon docked with the ISS—another commercial first in Vance’s narrative. NASA later provided further funds (Vance cites $440 million) to develop crewed Dragon.

At Tesla, a January 2010 Department of Energy loan agreement for 465million,aMay2010dealfortheformerNUMMIplantinFremont(withToyotainvestment),anda29June2010IPO(226 million raised) financed the Model S program. Tesla began shipping the Model S in 2012; that November Motor Trend named it Car of the Year, and Consumer Reports later gave it a 99/100 rating. Musk also helped conceive SolarCity, run by cousins Lyndon and Peter Rive, and sketched projects such as the Hyperloop while framing a multiplanetary and energy-transport “stack.”

Vance’s book closes while these careers are still open: barge landing tests, Model X, and AI concerns appear in the epilogue rather than as finished chapters.
