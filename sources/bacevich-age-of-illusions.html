Overview

The Age of Illusions is Bacevich’s interpretive history of the United States from the end of the Cold War through Donald Trump’s election in 2016. The book opens on John Updike’s Rabbit Angstrom asking, “Without the Cold War, what’s the point of being an American?” and takes that question as the problem the post–Cold War elite never answered. Bacevich argues that after 1989–91 an “intoxicated elite” promulgated a four-part consensus—(1) globalized neoliberalism, (2) global leadership as euphemism for hegemony or empire, (3) freedom redefined as personal autonomy and maximized choice, and (4) presidential supremacy with quasi-monarchical expectations—and that ordinary Americans repudiated that consensus by elevating Trump. The Baldwin epigraph and closing frame stress promises made and not kept.

For this United States 1990–present wiki the source is central to elite foreign-policy ideology after the Cold War, globalization and inequality, perpetual war and the all-volunteer force, the enlargement of presidential power, and the 2016 election as a domestic rupture comparable (in Bacevich’s own comparison) to the fall of the Berlin Wall. Earlier chapters recover postwar “bargain” and Cold War culture as background; the narrative spine is 1989–2016.

Narrative Source Flow

Introduction

Lines 63–246. Updike’s Rabbit question as still unanswered; Cold War as state of mind by the 1980s. Elite confidence after victory yields a four-element consensus: globalized neoliberalism (planetary unconstrained corporate capitalism and open movement of goods, capital, ideas, and people as engines of wealth); global leadership/hegemony/empire (unchallengeable military power to police a postcolonial yet implicitly imperial order favorable to American interests and to enforce globalization); freedom as autonomy (moral prohibitions obsolete, constraints removed, choice maximized, underwritten by order and abundance); presidential supremacy (Oval Office as quasi-monarchical center, coequal branches eroded in practice). Operating-system purpose: cement U.S. primacy and present the American way of life as humankind’s destiny. Brief American Epoch fails: inequality, habitual war, nihilistic empowerment, sequential presidential disappointments, implosion in November 2016. Trump as protest candidate and protest vote; 2016 paired with Berlin Wall as dual milestones. Baldwin theme: promises unkept, expectations unfulfilled.

Al, Fred, and Homer’s America—and Mine

Lines 247–838. Parallel births of Donald Trump (June 1946, wealthy New York real-estate family) and Bacevich (1947, Normal, Illinois; GI Bill parents). Shared postwar place defined by identifiable propositions—the postwar bargain of abundance, security, and status for those inside its terms. Culture of Al, Fred, and Homer (Cold War Everyman figures) and the author’s military-family path. Intelligentsia from the 1950s–80s challenging feasibility and desirability of postwar expectations. End of the postwar bargain; ground prepared for a new post–Cold War consensus as the era dawns. Last pre-consensus American war service and presidency as markers of transition.

The End of History!

Lines 839–1414. Cold War after early crises managed rather than passionately waged; mutual interest in avoiding Armageddon after Cuba 1962. Freedom as enduring keyword of American political discourse even as fads among the intelligentsia come and go. Territorial and resource competition as underlying aim of the bipolar struggle. Collapse of the Soviet project and Western triumphalism. Francis Fukuyama (1989) cast as godfather of a secular theology that would shape American attitudes and actions for decades—intellectual charter for the post–Cold War consensus.

Kicking 41 to the Curb

Lines 1415–1811. Trump’s early-1990s business and marital crises and flexible recovery without principle. George H. W. Bush’s brief post–Cold War presidency and the Gulf War moment. 1992 insurgencies: Patrick Buchanan’s challenge inside the Republican Party and H. Ross Perot’s independent campaign as early resistance before the consensus fully hardens. Election of Bill Clinton and foreshadowing of later populist rupture; resistance forming even as the new order is built.

Glimpsing the Emerald City

Lines 1812–2816. Oz metaphor for the yellow-brick path of post–Cold War expectation—smooth road, wishes seemingly near fulfillment. 1990s globalization boom, technology optimism, and the seamless blending of technology and skill in a vision of surgical, frictionless, postmodern warfare that keeps the average citizen as spectator and removes constraints on the use of U.S. military power. Construction and naturalization of the four-part consensus; claim that history itself demands submission and that no real alternative exists.

Bedfellows

Lines 2817–4278. Dual sources of presidential legitimacy: democratic election and inflated expectations of the president as supreme leader. Administrations of Clinton, George W. Bush, and Obama treated as successive managers (and testers) of the consensus rather than as fundamental alternatives to one another. September 11 and the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq; Cheney and turn-of-the-century imperial revival; rapid decisive victory imagined as object lesson. Continuities of neoliberal globalization, global military posture, empowerment rhetoric, and presidential centrality across party lines. Obama White House Correspondents’ Dinner skewering of Trump; “last laugh” foreshadowing.

State of the Union

Lines 4279–4876. America at the quarter-century mark of the post–Cold War era (2016); expected Hillary Clinton vs. Jeb Bush dynastic contest. Inequality, hollowed institutions, college as mobility ritual versus armed service as risk borne by others (Pat Tillman as rare counter-example). Social and political conditions that Trump, more than rivals, translated into votes—“the moment met the man.”

Plebiscite

Lines 4877–5750. 2016 election as unprecedented in American political history: least qualified of nearly two dozen candidates wins. Trump as composite of prior presidential vices and as inverse of Coolidge’s reticence. Republican field (including Rubio’s Reagan-vs-Carter template aimed at Obama) fails to hold the consensus line. Election math and non-voters; insistence that the preceding quarter century gave Trump’s supporters ample motivation even if the outcome is lamented.

Attending to Rabbit’s Question

Lines 5751–6548. Shock among protectors of the post–Cold War outlook at Clinton’s defeat; New York Times and elite reaction. Diagnosis of consensus failure and unfinished business of Rabbit’s question—what is the point of being an American without the Cold War? Continuing global security commitments (alliances, Gulf partners, NATO eastern flank) despite Trump–Putin optics. Closing charge: in a nation ostensibly of, by, and for the people, responsibility for change rests with the people; shirking it paves the way for more Trumps—or someone worse.

Coverage Guide

Post–Cold War elite consensus (core thesis)

Introduction and Chapters 2, 4, and 8 state and test the four elements—globalized neoliberalism, global leadership/empire, autonomy-freedom, presidential supremacy—and the claim that 2016 repudiated them. Primary mining ground for ideology pages.

From postwar bargain to 1989–91 rupture

Chapter 1 and early Chapter 2 recover Cold War culture, the postwar social bargain, and Fukuyama’s “end of history” as prologue to the wiki period.

1992 as early dissent

Chapter 3 (Buchanan, Perot, defeat of Bush 41, Clinton’s rise) for pre-consensus resistance and party fracture.

Globalization, tech optimism, and military postmodernism

Chapter 4 for 1990s Emerald City expectations, frictionless war doctrine, and naturalization of the consensus.

Continuity across Clinton–Bush–Obama

Chapter 5 for presidential supremacy, 9/11 wars, and bipartisan bedfellows under the same operating system.

2016 campaign and election

Chapters 6–7 for social conditions, candidate field, and Trump as protest vehicle.

After the shock / Rabbit unanswered

Chapter 8 for elite reaction and Bacevich’s normative close (still the author’s argument, not wiki editorial voice).
