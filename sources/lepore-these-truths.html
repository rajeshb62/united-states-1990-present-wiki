Overview

These Truths is Lepore’s one-volume political history of the United States, built around the proposition that the American experiment rests on a set of “truths” held to be self-evident—political equality, natural rights, and the sovereignty of the people—and that the nation’s history is the record of how those truths have been argued, denied, measured, broadcast, and disrupted. The working extract opens at Part One (front matter and preface are not present in this pandoc plain file); Locke’s line “In the beginning, all the World was America” heads Part One. Four parts and sixteen numbered chapters run from 1492 to the early twenty-first century; an Epilogue (“The Question Addressed”) returns to Hamilton’s Federalist question whether societies can establish good government by reflection and choice.

For this United States 1990–present wiki the densest period chapters are Fifteen (Battle Lines) through the close of the century and the 2000 election, Sixteen (America, Disrupted) from 9/11 through Obama and Trump’s election, and the Epilogue. Parts One–Three and Chapters 13–14 remain full-work duty: they supply the constitutional, technological, and rights-history armature Lepore carries into the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries.

Narrative Source Flow

Part One — THE IDEA (1492–1799)

One — The Nature of the Past

Lines 17–990. Columbus’s lost diary and Las Casas’s copies; the uneven historical record (letters, DNA, Facebook pages, hearing transcripts). Taíno songs, laws, and collapse after contact; origin stories across peoples; geological and fossil deep time; human migration into the Americas. History as what remains—and what was silenced.

Two — The Rulers and the Ruled

Lines 991–2472. Indigenous political worlds and European conquest and settlement; rulers and ruled in empires and colonies; early patterns of labor, land, and dominion that frame later constitutional claims.

Three — Of Wars and Revolutions

Lines 2473–3805. Opens on Benjamin Lay, the diminutive Quaker radical against slavery. Imperial wars, revolutionary politics, and the moral and political crisis of bondage within a language of liberty.

Four — The Constitution of a Nation

Lines 3806–5308. Madison arrives early in Philadelphia, May 1787; notes on republics; framing, ratification struggles, and the written Constitution as a national instrument—and as a document that postpones as much as it settles.

Part Two — THE PEOPLE

Five — A Democracy of Numbers

Lines 5328–6609. While Federalists and Anti-Federalists fight the Constitution in print, Adams in London and Jefferson in Paris argue at a distance. Census, representation, parties, and the quantitative machinery of popular government in the early republic.

Six — The Soul and the Machine

Lines 6610–8194. Maria W. Stewart walks a manuscript to the Liberator. Religious awakenings, reform, race, and the mechanical and spiritual transformations of the antebellum decades—souls fired with liberty beside engines of production and print.

Seven — Of Ships and Shipwrecks

Lines 8195–9633. Abel Upshur’s death on the USS Princeton (1844) and the fate of the Union turning on Texas. Expansion, annexation, party fracture, and the ship of state heading toward wreck.

Eight — The Face of Battle

Lines 9634–10883. Photography as historical evidence that traps time and does not discriminate between rich and poor. Civil War as lived and recorded experience; Lincoln, emancipation, and the face of modern battle.

Part Three — THE STATE

Nine — Of Citizens, Persons, and People

Lines 10899–12725. What is a citizen? Lincoln’s attorney general cannot find a satisfactory definition in 1862; 1866 and Reconstruction rewrite the terms. Citizens, persons, and people in law after slave emancipation—and the limits of those categories.

Ten — Efficiency and the Masses

Lines 12726–14944. Walter Lippmann in pinstripes; from philosophy student of James and Santayana to reporter. Progressive-era efficiency, mass public, expertise, and the problem of democratic knowledge in an industrial society.

Eleven — A Constitution of the Air

Lines 14945–16847. Hoover as “Master of Emergencies” and moral responsibility in business (1928 campaign). Depression, radio, and the new aerial constitution of mass communication and national administration.

Twelve — The Brutality of Modernity

Lines 16848–18482. 1939 World’s Fair on Queens ash heaps; Perisphere and the display of political and technological progress. War, totalitarianism, and the brutal edge of the modern state through World War II’s end.

Part Four — THE MACHINE

Thirteen — A World of Knowledge

Lines 18500–20992. Hiroshima morning via Hersey’s Hiroshima clerk Toshiko Sasaki; the “end of time” and the nuclear age. Cold War knowledge systems, facts versus truth (Weaver’s Ideas Have Consequences), McCarthyism, and the long march of equality as abiding political hope rather than a single case.

Fourteen — Rights and Wrongs

Lines 20993–23119. Nixon–Khrushchev kitchen-debate optics (1959); civil rights and the rights revolution; party sexism and conservative women’s revolt; Vietnam-to-Watergate arc closing as Nixon’s helicopter leaves and the Mall’s dream speech still echoes.

Fifteen — Battle Lines

Lines 23120–25878. Betty Ford and Morley Safer on equal rights and abortion (1975). Culture-war and party realignment through Reagan, cable and talk, Fairness Doctrine fights (ratings over editorial “elites” as arbiters of truth), Bork, Gingrich-era polarization, Clinton years, impeachment, and the 2000 Florida recount. Closes as Bush takes office: “house divided,” houses newly wired for the Internet, towers about to fall.

Sixteen — America, Disrupted

Lines 25879–28314. 9/11 morning on live television; wars, security state, and financial crisis; Facebook/Twitter-era public sphere; Obama’s election and “better history”; disruption as business and political keyword; polarization; Trump’s rise. Closes on the 2016 election nearly rending the nation, stoking fears and doubts about democracy—remorse and remedy deferred.

Epilogue — The Question Addressed

Lines 28315–28481. Returns to Philadelphia’s constitutional summer and Hamilton’s question of reflection and choice versus accident and force. Climate warmed since 1787; Trump and Paris accord; ice and rising seas. American experiment unfinished: revolution vs chaos, universal rights vs particularism, hierarchy of wealth, immigration, liberty in a land of slavery. Monument wars and Charlottesville. Liberalism’s post–civil-rights settlement and conservative counter-history (Bork, Buckley, Gingrich’s 1607–1965 fantasy). Populist nostalgia at home and abroad. Ship-of-state allegory: liberals failing to trim sails, conservatives burning planks and the mast of truth. Charge to a new generation to rebuild and learn to navigate by the stars.

Coverage Guide

Founding truths and method (wiki-wide armature)

Ch. 1 (historical record and silence); Ch. 4 (Constitution); Epilogue (Hamilton’s question restated). Use when entities/concepts need Lepore’s framing language—equality, sovereignty, consent—without importing later scholarly consensus labels.

Slavery, citizenship, Civil War, Reconstruction

Chs. 3, 7–9 for bondage within liberty, war as recorded fact, and the legal invention of national citizenship.

Mass public, media, and technology

Chs. 6, 10–13 for print/religion/machines; Lippmann and efficiency; radio “constitution of the air”; nuclear and knowledge systems. Chs. 15–16 for cable, Internet, social platforms, and disruption.

Rights revolution and culture war

Ch. 14–15: civil rights to Ford/Safer, abortion, ERA-adjacent fights, party realignment, Fairness Doctrine, courts vs elections.

1990–present (primary period mining)

  • Ch. 15 close: Gingrich, Clinton, impeachment, 2000 election, Internet wiring.
  • Ch. 16 entire: 9/11, Iraq and security, financial crisis, Obama, social media, Trump 2016.
  • Epilogue: climate, monuments, populism, truth and the ship of state, charge to the next generation.

Full-work duty

Do not skip Parts One–Three when mining “these truths,” constitutional structure, or long arcs Lepore explicitly carries forward; period pages should still trace claims to the chapter that states them.
