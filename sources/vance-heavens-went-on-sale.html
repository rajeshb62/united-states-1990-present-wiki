Period-Overlap Note

HOME wiki is United States 1990–present. The book’s core action (post–Cold War military space frustration → PhoneSat/Planet → Rocket Lab Electron → Astra → Firefly) sits squarely inside the period. New Zealand childhood/shed years for Peter Beck and Ukraine/Belarus backstories for Max Polyakov and Artiom Anisimov are origin prologue for U.S.-facing New Space companies, customers, and regulators. No sister-period copy required unless a future mining pass wants pre-1990 rocket-pioneer epigraph context alone.

Overview

When the Heavens Went on Sale is Vance’s five-year, multi-continent immersion in the New Space / smallsat launch boom that follows SpaceX’s proof that private rocketry can work. The book is explicitly not a SpaceX biography: Falcon 1 on Kwajalein opens as prologue myth, then the narrative tracks Planet Labs’ Earth-imaging constellation; Rocket Lab and Peter Beck’s Electron; Chris Kemp’s Stealth→Astra grind toward daily small launches; and Tom Markusic / Max Polyakov’s Firefly drama under U.S. national-security pressure. Framing thesis: low Earth orbit as a coming “computing shell” / mass-transit highway, powered by a shared hallucination of founders, welders, DARPA, and SPACs.

For this United States 1990–present wiki the source is central to post-Shuttle commercial space, Silicon Valley hardware culture at NASA Ames, Pentagon “responsive space,” venture-funded rocketry, and the politics of foreign capital in U.S. launch. EPUB structure: Dedication; Preface; Prologue; four titled parts (The Great Computer in the Sky; The Peter Beck Project; Ad Astra; The Maddest Max) totaling thirty-three numbered chapters; Epilogue; Thanks and Acknowledgments; Index; Photo Section; About the Author; Also by; Copyright; About the Publisher.

Narrative Source Flow

Prologue — A Shared Hallucination

Lines 143–849. September 2008 Falcon 1 success on Kwajalein as the door-opening myth; Musk/Bezos as billionaire unlockers; then the wider New Space cast—Planet, Rocket Lab/Electron, and a swarm of underfunded small-rocket hopefuls—betting that frequent cheap rides change the LEO economy from scarce railways to mass transit. Thesis close: space as shared hallucination, mythology plus capitalism, with Pete Worden as early military catalyst and comedy/tragedy ahead for the main companies.

Part: The Great Computer in the Sky (Planet Labs)

Ch. 1 — When Doves Fly

Lines 850–1187. February 2017 Chennai: Robbie Schingler and Planet Labs’ record 104-satellite PSLV ride (eighty-eight Planet “Doves”); constellation thesis—cheap shoebox cameras photographing the whole Earth daily and selling imagery as an online service; company survival stakes and national-pride Indian launch framing.

Ch. 2 — Space Force

Lines 1188–1375. Brigadier General Simon “Pete” Worden: Office of Strategic Influence fiasco after 9/11; Defense/DARPA frustration with Boeing–Lockheed launch scarcity; early SpaceX Falcon 1 contract and Kwajalein oversight; “responsive space” agenda that seeds later smallsat/small-rocket demand.

Ch. 3 — Welcome, Lord Vader

Lines 1376–2402. Worden takes NASA Ames; Silicon Valley’s uncelebrated past vs Ames as temple; PhoneSat and DIY satellite culture; “Pete’s Kids” attract Will Marshall, Robbie Schingler, Chris Kemp, and allied misfits into the Ames orbit that will birth Planet and later Astra connections.

Ch. 4 — The Rainbow Mansion

Lines 2403–2737. 2006 Cupertino commune at 21677 Rainbow Drive: Marshall, Schingler, Jessy Kate Cowan-Sharp, Kemp, and others invent communal living next to Ames; Rainbow Mansion as incubator salon for PhoneSat ideas and the future Planet Labs founding circle.

Ch. 5 — Phoning Home

Lines 2738–3267. Will Marshall’s English countryside childhood, risk philosophy, nature/conservation bent; path into NASA Ames science and the PhoneSat project with Chris Boshuizen—consumer phones as satellite guts.

Ch. 6 — The Birth of a Planet

Lines 3268–3793. Spreadsheet ranking of smallsat missions at the Rainbow Mansion; imagery wins; Planet Labs founding logic—many cheap short-lived cameras vs few billion-dollar buses; Cosmogia / Planet brand and the bid to democratize Earth observation.

Ch. 7 — The Great Computer in the Sky

Lines 3794–4560. Payoff chapter: Planet imagery in open-source intelligence (Decker Eveleth and Chinese missile-silo hunt, 2021); daily global record as geopolitical and commercial infrastructure; closes the Planet arc as “great computer in the sky.”

Part: The Peter Beck Project (Rocket Lab)

Ch. 8 — Big, If True

Lines 4561–4904. Vance in Auckland 2018; awkward Musk phone call (post–Elon Musk biography chill) while reporting Rocket Lab; Beck introduced as the other private company regularly orbiting satellites for customers.

Ch. 9 — A Boy and His Shed

Lines 4905–5255. Peter Beck childhood in Invercargill, gearhead New Zealand, Burt Munro lore; shed tinkerer origin myth for the future Electron founder.

Ch. 10 — You Just Stick It Between Your Legs and Pray

Lines 5256–5573. Number 8 wire mentality vs Beck’s ambition; Fisher & Paykel years and extreme DIY engineering that foreshadows Rocket Lab methods.

Ch. 11 — I Expected More from You, America

Lines 5574–5899. Yacht titanium-doorknob absurdity; Industrial Research Limited (IRL) Auckland; Beck’s U.S. contacts and disappointment with American aerospace bureaucracy; decision path toward building a Kiwi rocket company.

Ch. 12 — “You Fucking Beauty!”

Lines 5900–6439. Sounding rockets first; Rocket Lab founding; Ātea-1 and the ecstatic first reaches-space moment; Mark Rocket early money and the shoestring company that almost nobody notices.

Ch. 13 — The Military Is Not So Bad

Lines 6440–6783. Silicon Valley ignores Beck; DARPA/U.S. military does not—responsive-space interest in Ātea tech, propellants, and composite lightness; defense contracts keep Rocket Lab alive.

Ch. 14 — Enter Electron

Lines 6784–7602. 2013 California fundraising grind ($5M ask after a painful earlier dilution); pitch that tens of thousands of smallsats need frequent launch; Electron design, Mahia launch site, carbon-fiber vehicle; path from skepticism to orbital success.

Ch. 15 — You’ve Got Our Attention

Lines 7603–8225. 2018 unicorn raise (~140M;>1B valuation); Darth Vader HQ aesthetics; industrial Electron factory; Rocket Lab as SpaceX’s smallsat-frequency counterpart and competitive pressure on later Astra chapters.

Part: Ad Astra (Astra / Chris Kemp)

Ch. 16 — Let’s Make a Lot of Fucking Rockets

Lines 8226–8818. Rumors of SoMa rocket maker Ventions / Adam London; DoD/DARPA tiny-rocket contracts; Chris Kemp’s Stealth Space rebirth of the Ventions tech toward daily cheap launch from Alameda.

Ch. 17 — Chris Kemp on Chris Kemp, Spring 2017

Lines 8819–9200. Method chapter: long first-person Kemp monologue (Alabama childhood, Space Camp, start-up résumé, Nebula lessons); Vance’s “hear them talk” immersion contract for the Astra arc.

Ch. 18 — The Grind

Lines 9201–9745. Alameda former navy base; aluminum simplicity vs Rocket Lab carbon fiber; five hand-built engines; city-regulation skirmishes; countdown-clock culture of building a rocket in a year.

Ch. 19 — Party Like You Mean It

Lines 9746–9943. Stealth Space renames to Astra (Oct 2017); recruiters and suppliers force the rebrand; factory party celebrating a whole rocket on the floor.

Ch. 20 — Your Friendly Neighborhood Fog Monster

Lines 9944–10223. First upright outdoor tests; Chris Thompson (ex-SpaceX) joins; fog/noise over Orion Street; neighbors, Pottery Barn Outlet, and the ICBM-in-the-backyard vibe.

Ch. 21 — Not-So-Stealth Space

Lines 10224–10556. Factory expansion and Alaska first-flight plan; iterate-don’t-perfect philosophy; Ventions tech as head start; tension between software-speed culture and aerospace risk.

Ch. 22 — Northern Exposure

Lines 10557–11182. Kodiak / Pacific Spaceport Complex campaign; Rocket 1 launch attempt atmosphere (including Vance’s stripper-plane digression); Alaska as Astra’s proving ground.

Ch. 23 — Rocket 2

Lines 11183–11817. Sparse data from Rocket 1; Kemp’s optimistic investor spin; Rocket Lab orbital pressure and temporary Electron snag; Frankenmachine vs polished Electron race.

Ch. 24 — It’s a Job

Lines 11818–12096. RV park outside the Alameda factory; Les Martin, Matthew Flanagan, and cynical gearhead labor culture; Bay Area housing costs as aerospace subplot.

Ch. 25 — The Reset Button

Lines 12097–12608. Commercial rocket failure psychology vs government programs; explosions as deflation of founder exceptionalism; Astra reset after catastrophic attempts.

Ch. 26 — Cash on Fire

Lines 12609–12857. Rocket 3 / mobile-launcher destruction; helium mishap lore; COVID supply and LOX shortages; Pentagon “essential” designation; cash burn while still unproven.

Ch. 27 — It Makes Sense. Right?

Lines 12858–13229. February 2021 Astra SPAC / Nasdaq listing in the covid SPAC bubble; Wall Street hype vs launch reality; Kemp’s public-markets bet closing the Astra narrative arc.

Part: The Maddest Max (Firefly)

Ch. 28 — On Passion

Lines 13230–13490. Max Polyakov at the Texas Beer Barn; ~$200M personal plunge into Firefly Aerospace; Cedar Park / Firefly Farm introduction; Ukrainian-Silicon Valley odd-couple energy with Tom Markusic.

Ch. 29 — God Told Me to Do It

Lines 13491–14210. Tom Markusic origin: Mantua, Ohio; farm/machine “essence of engineering”; NASA/SpaceX/Blue Origin résumé; religious mission framing and Firefly’s first life/death spiral before Polyakov money.

Ch. 30 — Full Attack

Lines 14211–14946. Artiom Anisimov (Belarus→space law→Polyakov orbit); rescue/rebuild of Firefly; Ukraine industrial ties; full-attack capitalization and governance after near-collapse.

Ch. 31 — These Rockets: They’re Expensive

Lines 14947–15394. Menlo Park mansion / Oktoberfest; burn rate and investor theater; Vandenberg path; cost reality of trying to be the next orbital small-launcher.

Ch. 32 — Limits

Lines 15395–15593. Engine-test barbecue and Polyakov’s rage at missed deadlines; founder–workforce collision; limits of passion capital and schedule optimism.

Ch. 33 — Flameout

Lines 15594–15877. December 2020: Treasury/Air Force/NASA pressure; Polyakov forced off board and toward selling control as Firefly nears Vandenberg launch; badge downgraded to “Escort only”; national-security politics as flameout of the odd couple’s governance.

Epilogue

Lines 15878–16324. LeoLabs as commercial LEO “air traffic control”; Starlink/OneWeb/Planet debris and collision alerts; SpaceX dominance restated; SPAC hangover and 2022 risk-off markets. Character updates: Polyakov pushed out then watching Firefly’s successful second launch from afar; Markusic later ousted by professional investors; Polyakov’s Ukraine-war imagery pipeline; Planet/Rocket Lab/Astra/Firefly futures under competitive and capital pressure; closing “look up” return to the Alan Moore epigraph.

Chs. 1–7 for Ames/PhoneSat → Rainbow Mansion → Dove constellation → open-source intel payoff.

Military / DARPA responsive space

Chs. 2–3 and 13 for Worden, DARPA, and why the Pentagon bankrolled weird small rockets.

Rocket Lab / Peter Beck

Chs. 8–15 for shed-to-unicorn Electron and Mahia launch cadence.

Astra / Chris Kemp

Chs. 16–27 for Ventions→Stealth→Astra, Alameda grind, Kodiak failures, and SPAC exit.

Firefly / Polyakov–Markusic

Chs. 28–33 plus Epilogue for passion capital, Ukraine ties, and U.S. forced divestiture.
